\begin{abstract}
Urban microclimate and climate change affect both energy demand and indoor overheating in buildings. Building stock transition strategies also introduce environmental impacts through the production, replacement, and disposal of materials and technical systems. This study links these processes for the historic city centre of Antwerp, Belgium. The assessment comprises three stages: microclimate and future climate modelling, physics-based building performance simulation, and prospective Life Cycle Assessment (LCA). Level of Detail~2 CityGML geometries are converted into EnergyPlus epJSON models to calculate heating demand and hourly operative temperatures. Historical reference weather is compared with local microclimate weather, followed by future climate conditions under SSP1-2.6 and SSP3-7.0. Preliminary simulations indicate 21\% more indoor overheating hours and 10\% lower annual heating demand with microclimate weather than with historical reference weather. These results indicate that lower heating demand can coincide with greater indoor overheating. The prospective LCA evaluates the embodied impacts of building stock transition strategies alongside changes in operational impacts. The assessment examines whether strategies that reduce energy demand also limit indoor overheating. Their environmental performance is compared under future climate and energy-supply scenarios.

\section{Introduction}

Climate change and the Urban Heat Island (UHI) effect influence the outdoor conditions experienced by urban buildings. These conditions affect heat transfer through the building envelope and the potential for heat removal by ventilation. Assessing indoor overheating requires dynamic building performance simulation with weather data that represent the local climate~\cite{Ma2023Toolchain,Morales2025Review}.

Urban Building Energy Modelling (UBEM) provides a basis for assessing energy demand across building stocks. Previous work converted CityJSON geometry into EnergyPlus models to assess summer operative temperatures in Antwerp~\cite{Morales2025CityJSON}. Extending this approach to future climate requires local weather projections and consistent assumptions about building operation. Evaluating building stock transition strategies also requires the environmental impacts of materials, technical systems, and energy supply.

This study links microclimate and future climate modelling, building performance simulation, and prospective Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) for Antwerp's historic city centre. Three questions are examined. First, how does local microclimate change heating demand and indoor overheating relative to historical reference weather? Second, how do building stock transition strategies perform under SSP1-2.6 and SSP3-7.0? Third, how do their embodied and operational impacts compare over the assessment period? The same buildings and transition strategies are carried through the three stages.

\subsection{Case study and building models}

The case study covers 1473 buildings in Antwerp's historic city centre, Sint-Andries. LoD~2 CityGML geometry provides building orientation, envelope surfaces, and roof form. Construction properties and operational inputs complete the information required for dynamic simulation.

The building models extend the earlier Antwerp workflow~\cite{Morales2025CityJSON}. That workflow converted CityJSON geometry into EnergyPlus epJSON files. Here, CityGML semantic surfaces are read directly. Surface geometry is checked for planarity, orientation, and valid openings. Construction assemblies are assigned from building typologies, using available building attributes~\cite{Loga2016TABULA}. A default construction is recorded where the required attributes are unavailable. Valid explicit openings are retained where supplied; other facades receive windows from the assigned window-to-wall ratio.

Floor-level thermal zones retain vertical variation in operative temperature. Buildings are grouped into block-level simulation files, with nearby buildings represented as context shading. Shared walls are treated as adiabatic boundaries. Inter-building longwave exchange remains outside the model. Context shading represents solar obstruction by surrounding buildings.

\subsection{Energy demand and overheating metrics}

EnergyPlus calculates the building heat balance and hourly thermal-zone outputs~\cite{Crawley2001EnergyPlus}. Heating is supplied by an ideal loads system at a constant setpoint of 21~$^\circ$C. Mechanical cooling is disabled in the baseline. Occupancy, internal gains, infiltration, shading operation, and window-opening controls are held constant across weather comparisons. Keeping these inputs constant allows the effect of weather to be assessed for each building.

Annual heating demand is calculated by summing sensible heating energy across thermal zones. Building-level demand is divided by the corresponding conditioned floor area and reported in kWh/m$^2$. Aggregated energy demand is reported for the complete simulated stock. Ideal loads represent thermal energy demand; conversion to delivered energy is required for the LCA.

Indoor overheating is assessed from hourly operative temperature during occupied hours from 1~May to 30~September. A fixed threshold of 26~$^\circ$C is applied. For thermal zone $z$, the exceedance percentage $p_{26,z}$ and overheating degree hours $D_{26,z}$ are calculated as
\begin{equation}
 p_{26,z}=100\frac{\sum_{h\in H_z}\mathbf{1}(T_{\mathrm{op},z,h}>26)}{|H_z|},
 \qquad
 D_{26,z}=\sum_{h\in H_z}\max(T_{\mathrm{op},z,h}-26,0)\Delta t,
 \label{eq:overheating}
\end{equation}
where $H_z$ contains occupied assessment hours, $T_{\mathrm{op},z,h}$ is operative temperature in degrees Celsius, and $\Delta t=1$~h. The indicator $\mathbf{1}$ equals one when the threshold is exceeded and zero otherwise. Exceedance percentage describes the frequency of overheating; overheating degree hours also account for its magnitude. These metrics describe temperature exposure at the modelled zoning resolution.

Overheating is calculated for each thermal zone before spatial aggregation. Floor-area-weighted exceedance is reported for each building, together with the maximum floor-level exceedance. District summaries retain the distribution across buildings and floor positions. This prevents building means from concealing the most exposed floors.

\section{Discussion and Conclusions}

The preliminary comparison indicates 21\% more overheating hours and 10\% lower heating demand when local microclimate weather is used. These changes support evaluating heating demand and indoor overheating together. Floor-level assessment retains temperature variation within buildings, while district totals describe aggregated energy demand.

The prospective LCA extends this comparison to the materials and technical systems required by building stock transition strategies. Their environmental performance depends on installation dates, replacement needs, delivered energy, and future production processes. The future-climate and LCA results are required to establish which strategies reduce environmental impacts while limiting indoor overheating.

The assessment remains conditional on assigned constructions, standardised operation, and the selected weather and inventory scenarios. Independent observations of outdoor and indoor conditions are needed to evaluate prediction accuracy. The proposed climate coupling also limits conclusions about feedback from building operation to the urban microclimate.
