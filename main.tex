\begin{abstract}
The combined impact of Urban heat islands (UHI) and climate change affects both energy demand and indoor overheating in buildings. Building stock transition strategies also introduce environmental impacts through the production, replacement, and disposal of materials and technical systems. This study links these processes for the historic city centre of Antwerp, Belgium. The assessment comprises three stages: microclimate and future climate modelling, physics-based building performance simulation, and prospective Life Cycle Assessment (LCA). Level of Detail~2 CityGML geometries are converted into EnergyPlus epJSON models to calculate heating demand and hourly operative temperatures. Historical reference weather is compared with local microclimate weather, followed by future climate conditions under SSP1-2.6 and SSP3-7.0. Preliminary simulations indicate 21\% more indoor overheating hours and 10\% lower annual heating demand with microclimate weather than with historical reference weather. These results indicate that lower heating demand can coincide with greater indoor overheating. The prospective LCA evaluates the embodied impacts of building stock transition strategies alongside changes in operational impacts. The assessment examines whether strategies that reduce energy demand also limit indoor overheating. Their environmental performance is compared under future climate and energy-supply scenarios.

\section{Introduction}

Climate change and the UHI effect influence the outdoor conditions experienced by urban buildings. These conditions affect heat transfer through the building envelope and dictate the thermal behaviour of the building stock. Indoor thermal comfort in cities can be assessed using dynamic building performance simulation with weather data that represent the local microclimate~\cite{Morales2025Review}. Urban Building Energy Modelling (UBEM) is the urban-level application of this simulation approach across building stocks, typically to assess energy demand and consumption. A previous work has extended this application to indoor thermal comfort assessment by converting CityGML/CityJSON geometries into EnergyPlus models to assess summer operative temperatures~\cite{Morales2025CityJSON}. Extending this approach to future climate requires local weather projections and consistent assumptions about building operation. 

Building stock transition strategies are being designed in cities to optimize both energy efficiency and climate resilience. Simulating indoor thermal comfort in cities allows heating and indoor overheating to be assessed under both local microclimate and future climate conditions. However, these strategies also introduce environmental impacts through the production, replacement, and disposal of materials and technical systems. Therefore, there is a need to assess environmental trade-offs across scales, which could be done by coupling urban assessment tools such as UBEM and urban-level LCA~\cite{PichardoVelazquez2026}.

This study applies this approach to the historic city centre of Antwerp, Belgium. The assessment comprises three stages: microclimate and future climate modelling, UBEM with indoor thermal comfort assessment, and prospective LCA.

Specifically, the objectives of this study are to: (1) characterise microclimate and future weather conditions under SSP1-2.6 and SSP3-7.0 relative to typical meteorological year (TMY) weather; (2) simulate heating demand and indoor operative temperatures under these weather conditions; (3) quantify embodied and operational impacts through prospective LCA; and (4) evaluate the trade-offs between energy demand, indoor overheating, and environmental impacts across building stock transition strategies.

\section{Physics-Based Simulations of Energy Demand and Indoor Overheating}
\label{sec:buildings}

\subsection{Case study and building models}

The case study covers 1473 buildings in Antwerp's historic city centre, Sint-Andries. LoD~2 CityGML geometry provides building orientation, envelope surfaces, and roof form. Construction properties and operational inputs complete the information required for dynamic simulation.

In this study, the available 3D CityGML models coming from the Antwerp municipality are converted to EnergyPlus epJSON files using an extended translation pipeline from a previous work~\cite{Morales2025CityJSON} for simulations of energy and indoor temperature. Surface geometry is checked for planarity, orientation, and valid openings. Construction assemblies are assigned using available building attributes for the Belgian building stock~\cite{Loga2016TABULA}. A default construction is recorded where the required attributes are unavailable. Valid explicit openings are retained where supplied; other facades receive windows from the assigned window-to-wall ratio. Buildings are grouped into block-level simulation files, with nearby buildings represented as context shading. Shared walls of neighbouring buildings are treated as adiabatic boundaries. Context shading represents solar obstruction by surrounding buildings.

\subsection{Energy demand and overheating metrics}

EnergyPlus calculates the building heat balance and hourly thermal-zone outputs~\cite{Crawley2001EnergyPlus}. Heating is supplied by an ideal loads system at a constant setpoint of 21~$^\circ$C. Mechanical cooling is disabled in the baseline. Occupancy, internal gains, infiltration, shading operation, and window-opening controls are held constant across weather comparisons. Keeping these inputs constant allows the effect of weather to be assessed for each building.

Annual heating demand is calculated by summing sensible heating energy across thermal zones. Building-level demand is divided by the corresponding conditioned floor area and reported in kWh/m$^2$. Aggregated energy demand is reported for the complete simulated stock. Ideal loads represent thermal energy demand; conversion to delivered energy is required for the LCA.

Indoor overheating is assessed from hourly operative temperature during occupied hours from 1~May to 30~September. A fixed threshold of 26~$^\circ$C is applied. For thermal zone $z$, the exceedance percentage $p_{26,z}$ and overheating degree hours $D_{26,z}$ are calculated as
\begin{equation}
 p_{26,z}=100\frac{\sum_{h\in H_z}\mathbf{1}(T_{\mathrm{op},z,h}>26)}{|H_z|},
 \qquad
 D_{26,z}=\sum_{h\in H_z}\max(T_{\mathrm{op},z,h}-26,0)\Delta t,
 \label{eq:overheating}
\end{equation}
where $H_z$ contains occupied assessment hours, $T_{\mathrm{op},z,h}$ is operative temperature in degrees Celsius, and $\Delta t=1$~h. The indicator $\mathbf{1}$ equals one when the threshold is exceeded and zero otherwise. Exceedance percentage describes the frequency of overheating; overheating degree hours also account for its magnitude. These metrics describe temperature exposure at the modelled zoning resolution.

Overheating is calculated for each thermal zone before spatial aggregation. Floor-area-weighted exceedance is reported for each building, together with the maximum floor-level exceedance. District summaries retain the distribution across buildings and floor positions. This prevents building means from concealing the most exposed floors.

\section{Discussion and Conclusions}

The preliminary comparison indicates 21\% more overheating hours and 10\% lower heating demand when local microclimate weather is used. These changes support evaluating heating demand and indoor overheating together. Floor-level assessment retains temperature variation within buildings, while district totals describe aggregated energy demand.

The prospective LCA extends this comparison to the materials and technical systems required by building stock transition strategies. Their environmental performance depends on installation dates, replacement needs, delivered energy, and future production processes. The future-climate and LCA results are required to establish which strategies reduce environmental impacts while limiting indoor overheating.

The assessment remains conditional on assigned constructions, standardised operation, and the selected weather and inventory scenarios. Independent observations of outdoor and indoor conditions are needed to evaluate prediction accuracy. The proposed climate coupling also limits conclusions about feedback from building operation to the urban microclimate.
